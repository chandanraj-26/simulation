📚 Doppler Effect & Acoustic Wavefront Theory

1. Christian Doppler's Discovery (1842)

In 1842, Austrian physicist Christian Doppler postulated that the observed frequency of a wave depends on the relative motion between the source and the observer. In 1845, Dutch scientist C. H. D. Buys Ballot experimentally confirmed this by placing trumpet players on an open moving train flatcar!

2. General Doppler Formula

For sound waves propagating through a stationary medium at speed $v$:

f_obs = f₀ · (v ± v_obs) / (v ∓ v_source)

Sign convention:

  • Source approaches Observer: Use minus sign in denominator $\to (v - v_{\text{source}})$, causing $f_{\text{obs}} > f_0$ (pitch rises, wavefronts compressed).
  • Source recedes from Observer: Use plus sign in denominator $\to (v + v_{\text{source}})$, causing $f_{\text{obs}} < f_0$ (pitch drops, wavefronts dilated).
  • Wavelength in medium: $\lambda_{\text{compressed}} = (v - v_{\text{source}}) / f_0$.

3. Mach Number and the Sonic Barrier

The Mach Number ($M$) is defined as the ratio of source velocity to the speed of sound:

M = v_source / v_sound
  • Subsonic ($M < 1$): Wavefronts spread out in concentric rings that never touch or cross each other.
  • Sonic ($M = 1$): The source travels at the exact speed of the wavefronts it emits. All wavefronts pile up along a vertical boundary creating a shock wall of constructive interference!
  • Supersonic ($M > 1$): The source outruns its own sound. The expanding spherical wavefronts form a conical envelope called the Mach Cone.

4. Mach Cone Half-Angle Formula

sin(μ) = v_sound / v_source = 1 / M ⇒ μ = arcsin(1 / M)

As the aircraft flies faster (higher Mach number), the Mach cone becomes narrower and sharper. When this high-pressure cone sweeps across the ground, observers hear the sudden double-boom known as a sonic boom!
