Electromagnetic Induction — NCERT Theory

1. Historical Context:
In 1831, English scientist Michael Faraday discovered that electric currents can be produced in a closed circuit without a physical battery, solely by the relative motion of a magnetic field. Almost simultaneously, Heinrich Lenz formulated the directional rule governing the polarity of these induced currents.


2. Faraday's Law of Induction:
The magnitude of the electromotive force (EMF, 𝓔) induced in a coil of wire is directly proportional to the time rate of change of magnetic flux (ΦB) through the coil:

Faraday's Induction Equation:
𝓔 = −N · (dΦB / dt)

Where:
• N = Number of turns in the solenoid coil
• ΦB = Magnetic flux = B · A · cos(θ) (measured in Webers, Wb)
• t = Time in seconds (rate of change dΦ/dt in Wb/s or Volts)

3. Lenz's Law & Conservation of Energy:
The negative sign in Faraday's equation represents Lenz's Law: The polarity of the induced electromotive force is always such that it produces an electric current whose magnetic field opposes the change in magnetic flux that caused it.

  • Approaching North Pole: As a North pole approaches the coil, the facing end of the coil develops a North pole to repel the incoming magnet. Mechanical work must be done against this repulsive force to push the magnet in.
  • Receding North Pole: As the North pole is withdrawn, the facing end becomes a South pole to attract the magnet back, resisting its withdrawal.
  • Energy Conservation: The mechanical work done by the experimenter against these opposing magnetic forces is precisely converted into electrical energy in the coil and dissipated as heat ($I^2R$).

4. Effect of Soft Iron Core:
Inserting a ferromagnetic soft iron core inside the coil multiplies the magnetic field concentration by the relative permeability constant: Φiron = μr · Φair, generating dramatically higher induced voltages.
