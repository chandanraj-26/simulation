Physics Theory & Mathematical Derivations

1. Ohm's Law & Macroscopic Conduction

Discovered by German physicist Georg Simon Ohm (1827), Ohm's Law states that at constant temperature and physical dimensions, the electric current passing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its terminals:

V = I · R  ⇔  I = \frac{V}{R}  ⇔  R = \frac{V}{I}
Where V is potential difference (Volts, V), I is current (Amperes, A), and R is electrical resistance (Ohms, Ω).

In vector differential form, Ohm's law relates the current density J to the electric field E via electrical conductivity σ:

\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{E}
Where J = I / A (A/m²), E = V / L (V/m), and ρ is the material resistivity (Ω·m).

2. Drude Microscopic Model of Conduction

Paul Drude (1900) proposed that metals contain a free electron gas moving through a stationary lattice of positive metallic ions. Free electrons undergo rapid thermal motion ($v_{\text{thermal}} \approx 10^5\text{–}10^6\text{ m/s}$) in random directions with zero net drift without an applied field.

v_d = \frac{e \tau}{m_e} E = \frac{I}{n A e}
When voltage is applied, the electric field accelerates electrons. Successive collisions with vibrating lattice ions decelerate them, establishing an average steady drift velocity v_d (typically $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-5}$ m/s). Here $n \approx 8.5 \times 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$ (free electron density for Cu), $A$ is wire cross-section ($1\text{ mm}^2$), and $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.

3. Joule Heating & Thermal Resistance Drift

As drifting electrons scatter off lattice ions, kinetic energy is transferred to the lattice vibrations (phonons), heating the resistor:

P = I^2 R = V I = \frac{V^2}{R}
Dissipated thermal power $P$ causes temperature rise governed by Newton's law of cooling: $\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{P - h(T - T_0)}{C_{\text{th}}}$.
R(T) = R_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right]
As temperature $T$ rises, lattice ions vibrate with larger amplitude, increasing electron scattering cross-section and elevating resistance. This causes non-linear curvature in the V-I characteristic at high power, illustrating why Ohm's law strictly holds only at constant temperature.

4. Instrument Topologies & Laboratory Safety

Accurate laboratory measurements depend on proper instrument connection:

\text{Ammeter: } R_A \to 0\ \Omega \quad \text{(Connected in SERIES)}
Because an ammeter has near-zero internal resistance, placing it in parallel across a power supply creates a direct short circuit, producing catastrophic current surges and risking meter destruction or fires!
\text{Voltmeter: } R_V \to \infty\ \Omega \quad \text{(Connected in PARALLEL)}
A voltmeter possesses extremely high input impedance (~100 kΩ to 10 MΩ) so it draws negligible current. Placing it in series choke-blocks the circuit loop, dropping total current to near zero (open loop).
