📚 Chemical Kinetics & Arrhenius Theory

1. The Disappearing Cross Reaction

When sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid, insoluble elemental sulfur precipitates in colloidal form ($S_8$ rings), creating a milky yellow cloud that scatters light:

Na₂S₂O₃ (aq) + 2HCl (aq) → 2NaCl (aq) + SO₂ (g) + S (colloidal s) + H₂O (l)

Because the amount of sulfur required to obscure the black cross is fixed ($m_S = \text{constant}$), the reaction rate is inversely proportional to the time ($t$) taken for the cross to disappear:

Relative Rate ∝ 1 / t (s⁻¹)

2. Effect of Concentration (Reaction Order)

According to Collision Theory, increasing reactant concentration increases the frequency of collisions between reacting ions per unit volume per second. The rate law is:

Rate = k · [Na₂S₂O₃]¹ · [HCl]⁰

The reaction is strictly first-order with respect to thiosulfate, so doubling $[\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3]$ halves the time ($t$) required for extinction.

3. Effect of Temperature & The Arrhenius Equation

Rising temperature gives molecules higher kinetic energy ($E_k = \frac{3}{2}kT$), dramatically increasing the fraction of collisions that exceed the activation energy ($E_a$):

k = A · exp(-Ea / (R · T)) ⟹ ln(k) = ln(A) - (Ea / R) · (1 / T)

Plotting $\ln(1/t)$ vs. $1/T$ yields a straight line with slope $= -E_a / R$. Multiplying by $R = 8.314\text{ J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$ directly gives the activation energy of the reaction ($\approx 52\text{ kJ/mol}$).
