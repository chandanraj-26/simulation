Blood Grouping & Immunogenetics Lab Manual

1. The ABO Blood Group System

Discovered in 1900 by Karl Landsteiner, the ABO blood system is determined by the presence or absence of specific carbohydrate oligosaccharide antigens (A and B) anchored to erythrocyte membranes via the lipid precursor H substance.

Landsteiner's Law: If an agglutinogen (antigen) is present on a person's RBCs, the corresponding agglutinin (antibody) MUST be absent from their plasma, and vice versa.

2. The Rhesus (Rh) System & Factor D

The Rh system comprises over 50 antigens, with the transmembrane protein D antigen being the most clinically significant and highly immunogenic. Individuals possessing the D antigen are classified as Rh-positive (Rh+); those lacking it are Rh-negative (Rh-).

Unlike ABO antibodies which occur naturally without prior red cell exposure, Anti-D antibodies are typically IgG molecules that develop after Rh- individuals are sensitized by Rh+ blood during transfusion or fetomaternal hemorrhage (potentially causing Erythroblastosis Fetalis / Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Newborn).

3. Hemagglutination Mechanism

Commercial antisera contain high-affinity monoclonal antibodies (primarily pentameric IgM for Anti-A and Anti-B). Because each IgM molecule possesses 10 antigen-binding Fab sites, it bridges adjacent red blood cells, overcoming their natural electrostatic repulsive zeta potential and crosslinking them into macroscopic aggregates (clumping).

4. Blood Transfusion Compatibility Rules

  • Universal RBC Donor (O-): Lacks A, B, and Rh(D) antigens; safe to administer in emergency trauma transfusions before complete type-and-crossmatch.
  • Universal RBC Recipient (AB+): Possesses all major antigens; plasma contains neither anti-A nor anti-B agglutinins, allowing safe reception of any RBC unit.
