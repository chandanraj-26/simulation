Buffon's Needle — Mathematical Theory

1. Historical Context:
In 1777, French naturalist Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, posed the famous geometric probability question: "If a needle of length L is dropped randomly onto a floor ruled with parallel lines spaced at distance D, what is the probability that the needle crosses a line?"


2. Integral Derivation (for L ≤ D):
Let x be the perpendicular distance from the needle's midpoint to the nearest gridline (0 ≤ x ≤ D/2), and let θ be the acute angle formed by the needle with the parallel lines (0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2).

A needle crosses a line if and only if its half-length projected perpendicular to the lines exceeds x:

Line-Crossing Condition:
x ≤ (L / 2) · sin(θ)

Integrating over the uniform joint probability distribution of x and θ:

Probability Integral:
P = [ 1 / ((D/2) · (π/2)) ] ∫0π/2 (L / 2) · sin(θ) dθ
P = (4 / πD) · (L / 2) [−cos(θ)]0π/2
P = (2 · L) / (π · D)

3. Monte Carlo π Estimation:
By equating the theoretical probability P with the observed experimental hit ratio C / N (crossings / total drops):

C / N ≈ (2 · L) / (π · D)  ⇒  π ≈ (2 · L · N) / (D · C)

By the Law of Large Numbers, as total drops N → ∞, the experimental fraction converges to the exact theoretical probability, and the calculated ratio converges to π = 3.14159265...
