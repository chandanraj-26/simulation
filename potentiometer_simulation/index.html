Potentiometer Principles — Theoretical Derivations

1. Principle of the Potentiometer

A potentiometer consists of a long, uniform high-resistivity wire (Constantan or Manganin) stretched on a wooden board. When a steady current flows through a wire of uniform cross-section, the potential fall across any portion of the wire is directly proportional to its length:

V ∝ l ⇒ V = k · l

where k is the potential gradient of the wire ($k = V / L$ in $\text{V/cm}$).

2. Why Potentiometers Outperform Voltmeters

An ordinary voltmeter draws a small operating current from the cell under test, measuring terminal voltage $V = E - Ir$ rather than the true open-circuit EMF $E$. A potentiometer operates on the null-deflection principle, drawing zero current from the test cell at balance point, acting as an infinite-impedance ideal voltmeter.

3. Experiment 1: Comparison of EMFs of Two Cells

If $l_1$ and $l_2$ are the balancing lengths for a Leclanché cell ($E_1$) and Daniell cell ($E_2$) respectively:

E₁ = k · l₁   and   E₂ = k · l₂   ⇒   E₁ / E₂ = l₁ / l₂

4. Experiment 2: Internal Resistance of a Primary Cell

  1. With key K₂ open, the balancing length $l_1$ gives the true EMF:
    E = k · l₁
  2. With key K₂ closed through a known shunt resistance $R$, current is drawn, giving terminal voltage $V$:
    V = k · l₂
  3. Since $E = I(R + r)$ and $V = IR$, we have $E / V = (R + r) / R = 1 + r/R$:
    r = R · (l₁ − l₂) / l₂
